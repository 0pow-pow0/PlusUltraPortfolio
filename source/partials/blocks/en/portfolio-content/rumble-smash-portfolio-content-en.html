🎫 Event-driven as an objective

Before even talking about the actual challenges faced, I want discuss the persistent logic in every part of the game, used for practically all the scripts that animate the game. I won't go through the sections to re-explain what I wrote here, but in a different context, I will link directly to this section.

Practically the entire project adopts a pattern Pervasive Observer/Event-Driven as the main mechanism decoupling between systems. Every manager exposes UnityEvent public that UI, Camera, VFX and other managers subscribe in Start() without no direct reference between producer and consumer.

🧭 CONTEXT!

Dealing with multiple scripts that need to pass information to each other at a given moment it is a significant challenge. Let's take it the player example, it has to handle things like: Particles, Audio, UI Feedback, Graphical Feedback, Skeleton Animations and a thousand other different problem categories.

❓ WHY?

Why did I use an event-driven approach? Which ones are the advantages?

By exploiting event-driven design I obtain advantages such as: optimize the use of resources to the bare minimum, avoiding pooling altogether; avoid circular dependencies between scripts, reducing complexity; increase testability, the maintainability, the reusability and the flexibility of the code.

This way it is much easier to manage all the variables linked to the player, I can remove invasive graphic feedback During testing, add mocks to make it easier to build of certain features, disable physics, audio, etc.

⚽ The ball

Overall #FiniteStateMachine #ScriptableObjects

The ball was clearly one of the most important parts of the game, physics and feedback had to travel at the same frequency wave, otherwise the player could have had an experience too frustrating or not very intuitive. Let's analyze the peculiarities that make the rumble smash ball unique!

🧭 CONTEXT

I wanted the ball to have different behaviors based on the its speed, in order to make its behavior unique. At low velocity should have behaved like a normal ball, with standard physical parameters, but at medium and high speeds it would be became a breakout style ball, zero physical and complete conservation of its momentum at every collision, practically unstoppable, thus completely changing the approach of players to it and their playstyle.

For gameplay reasons it was also essential to be able distinguish ball speeds, for example, at high speeds could have scored more points, or as in this case, would have done more damage, since the doors have shields.

ℹ️
Nomenclature

For simplicity each range of speed at which the behavior varies, is defined stage.

Ball Stage

Features

Preview

Low Velocity (Ball Stage 1)

- Realistic physics with bounce coefficient similar to a bouncing ball.
- Damage minimal damage. (See the game design section to understand this point).
- Influence of gravity equal to 0.8 (normalized).

Medium Velocity (Ball Stage 2)

- Damage increased.
- Impact frames at collision with a wall, useful for providing seconds to think to the player and to make the experience more fluid.
- Zero influence of gravity.
- Near maximum rebound coefficient,
so that the conservation of motion is not maximum, therefore, it loses speed slightly.
- Different graphic feedback .

Max Velocity (Ball Stage 3)

- Maximum speed cap, cannot go beyond X speed, even if hit.
- The frames of impact last a little longer.
- Feedback different graphs .
- Damage maximum damage (See the game design section to understand this point).
- Coefficient of rebound to the maximum, therefore, total conservation of motion.
- Since it retains all its speed without it stop, the ball remains in this stage for X seconds, if a player hits it the timer resets. Upon expiration of timer its rebound coefficient decreases, so that it can fall below the minimum threshold of stage 3, thus returning to stage 2.

The architecture of the FSM allowed me to obtain a complete control over the states (stage) and parameters of the ball, if possible manage the initialization and update of parameter-dependent parameters states of the ball, this thanks to the "EnterState" functions, UpdateState & ExitState" of each FSM state, which they allowed me to act on all the parameters and recall the right functions with extreme ease, adapting to multiple contexts and necessity, as well as the states of the ball. Furthermore, I have a clear structure of the states of the ball and gain in terms of readability and maintainability.

Hitting the ball

🧭 CONTEXT

I wanted the feeling of the gameplay to be natural and fast, opt for consistent and realistic ball physics was not an option that would allow me to achieve this goal. So, I have opted to bend to physics as needed more arcade, Let's see how.

To maximize the speed of the gameplay I made sure that the ball completely retained its motion, therefore every shot received and communicated through an OnTriggerEnter, did not influence in any way the magnitude of its speed. For this I created a custom AddForce() method.

AddForce (Ball.cs) 
public void AddForce(Vector2 force, int chargeAdd)
{
    // ? --- Obtains direction of the force
    Vecotr2 forceDir = force.normalized;
    Vector2 linearVel = rb.linearVelocity;
    
    // ? --- Arcade babyyyyyy
    rb.linearVelocity = Vector2.zero;
    Vector2 resultingChargeForce =
        new Vector2
        (
            // Instantly changes direction without losing force
            forceDir.x * linearVel.magnitude +
            forceDir.x * chargeAdd,
            forceDir.y * linearVel.magnitude +
            forceDir.y* chargeAdd
        );
    rb.AddForce(resultingChargeForce, ForceMode2D.Impulse);
}

The calculation preserves the pre-existing speed module of the ball, using it as a component for the resulting vector final. The direction of the force received by the player comes instead normalized, since it only represents the direction of the blow inflicted, so I can use it to change the direction of motion of the ball without altering its accumulated speed . During the calculation the resulting vector is influenced by the power of player shot. See the section dedicated to the player.

❓ WHY?

Why did you choose this algorithm?

As mentioned, the reasons behind these choices lie in the feeling ending that I wanted to give to the game. Obviously realistic physics and bounces consistent with reality would have slowed down the game, moving away from my goal described in the GDD. The intent was that of creating a mixture of realistic physics during the internship initial of the ball, and arcade physics, which takes over the others stage.

The Sequencer
To be able to create such a system it was necessary for there to be a "conductor" who could manage different types of assets with ease, turning them on and off on command, the name of this script is "Sequencer". The Sequencer does nothing else than running a set of assets according to FIFO logic, does the former asset, as soon as its termination conditions come true, it passes to the next, until it has executed them all sequentially.

Sequencer execution logic

To ensure that the Sequencer could handle the transition between the different types of assets without knowing their behavior, each of them asset necessarily had to have as its parent a class known with methods common to all children, the father I am talking about takes his name of "Eventable". I was essentially exploiting MAINLY (there are influences from other patterns, such as the Command pattern and the state pattern, but it does not have all of them peculiarity, therefore it better fits into the TMDP) the "Template method design pattern" thanks to which I enclosed common methods among all children. I

Each "Eventable" has a variable that contains the state, being that the Sequencer will have to cycle between the various assets, it must also know when to do it and to know it it reads the variable EventableState

To summarize:

  • Template Method pattern: Sequencer call OnStart() → Update() every frame → OnEnd() when stay == QUIT

  • EventableState simple state machine: PENDING → START → UPDATE (loop) → QUIT (required by subclass) → ENDED

Each child of Eventable, however, is a ScriptableObject that represents an asset, each of which has behavior and parameters customized, such as: "Dialogue", "Cutscene", "Mission".

❓ WHY?

Why did I choose this structure for the Sequencer and Eventables?

The structure of the Sequencer is deliberately simple, the needs that this architecture had to satisfy were essential. I had the need to integrate many different assets together without complex conditional structures, therefore, this was the most way short. The template method pattern allowed me to abstract common methods useful for leveraging assets on time real, like the function Update() which has a beahviour different for each asset, for example, in the dialogues the beavhoiur is check each frame to verify user input to pass to the next line, or, for a mission, check every frame if the termination conditions are satisfied. Moving the behavior to the asset code itself which derives from Eventable the Sequencer manages them with extreme ease, without having any knowledge of the behaviour.

Why are Eventables SOs?

By exploiting SOs I was able to create a data-driven architecture, where I can take advantage of the integration in Inspector, reuse the assets and test them individually regardless of the scene. Furthermore, the OSs are easily editable by anyone (designer-proof!!!! 😎) adding and removing dialogues is has become a simple matter of clicks! Along with this, I can use the same asset with multiple Sequencers, independently from the scene it is in.

📦 Single-Entry-Point | Composition Root/Bootstrap Scene, Study case

Using this pattern is more because I wanted it study the usefulness and why my mind works better with gods pattern (ADHD be like). Furthermore, within the project there were some small problems related to temporal coupling, which could be solved like this.

❓ WHY

Why did you choose to use the SEP?

The SEP allowed me to: simplify the reading of the scene; by follow the initialization order very easily via code without being bombarded by multiple GameObjects; solve dependencies and temporal coupling problems easily; mi forced to categorize the objects on scene in order to then be able to instantiate from script (obtaining the obvious advantages of simple order); it allowed me to create a non-fake loading screen (not realized due to time constraints). Also, the real benefit is derived from deepening our knowledge and understanding, given that it is an industry-standard pattern.

🧭 CONTEXT

The intent was to create the SEP for the game build scenes, which contained over 100 GameObjects. References had to be easily replaceable with new components to stay up to date step of the project changes.

🛠️ HOW?

The way was simple, first of all I created prefabs of every single GameObject, serializing them consistently with their type e calculating their transform always based on the root of the scene, without manipulations due to the father's transforms, otherwise during the initialization the positions would be completely busted.

After that, I created the bootstrap script with all the useful references of the scene, this way if I wanted to change of references with alternative assets I could have done quietly. In the case of the UI, but as for all the others reference, for example, change a screen type with a template different it was just a matter of drag 'n drop.

Next, I divided the assets into categories and have them initialized in the following order

  1. Manager

  2. Entities

  3. Manager Entities Refs

  4. Level

  5. Manager Level Refs

  6. UI As you can see, the managers appear several times, since I preferred to split the initialization and configuration by prioritize it at the top of the list. Theoretically it is not useful, since objects looking for reference outside they do it themselves in the "Start()" function (good practices save us 🤗), when the configurations are already all completed by bootstrapping. But to avoid unnecessary debugging I have preferred this approach.

The result converges in these two simple functions:

Initiator and Start (Bootstrap) 
void Initiator()
{
    InitializeManagers();
    InitializeEntities();
    InitializeManagersEntitiesReferences();
    InitializeLevel();
    InitializeManagersLevelReferences();
    InitializeUI();
}

void Start()
{
    Initiator();
}

⬇️ Match Flow | State Pattern & FSM

🧭 CONTEXT

Well, now let's take the field, the players have to play, activate their devices, show the their skin in a special screen, wait the starting whistle, a few seconds and then... GOAL!!!!!!! Well, they scored, let's give them space to celebrate the their deeds. Enough of this, IN THE PLACES OF DEPARTURE, 3... 2... 1... GO, AGAIN! Three nil, well, victory overwhelming, wait the loser doesn't give up, he wants to challenge the again winner, that's good, don't give up, let's start again!

What was I talking about? Well, obviously, I'm talking about "Match Flow"! By "Match Flow" I mean a rigid structure that marks the phases of a game, each of them parts of the match flow must meet different requirements, for example, if we have just scored 3 goals, we end our match and we ask the player if he wants to play again, this happens in the phase "Match End" of the "Match Flow". At every goal, instead, we reset the position of the players and make them wait for the starting whistle.

Essentially, we are talking about a complicated problem related to events, we must ensure that each phase occurs only ed exclusively after another, each of these phases has a unique behavior, such as audio, graphics or of the logic itself, we must ensure that the gameplay action the "Match flow" respond accordingly. Furthermore, we want it to be flexible, if I want to rearrange the flow not I want to change the entire infrastructure. Well, the more approach makes sense is obviously an event-driven design!

I also decided to exploit the Singleton design pattern for the code dedicated to managing the "Match flow", to its internally there is event-driven logic. The class is called "**MatchManager".

❓ WHY

Why did you choose an event-driven design?

The reason lies in the ease of manipulation: if we can connect one phase to another, in cascade, via events, and connect the rest of the phases to the correct gameplay events, the match flow it would be very easy to change along with the order of appearance for more scripted phases.

Why did you choose the Singleton Design Pattern for the Match Manager?

Since the match manager is unique within the scene and does not others may exist, it is natural to think of the Singleton pattern to exploit its first peculiarity: uniqueness. Besides, I can reference the MatchManager to add listeners to events with further simplicity.

Before seeing the phases of a match, it is important to know that a match is structured into a maximum of 5 rounds, each of which ends as soon as a point is scored, the first to reach 3 wins! Programming - Post Mortem - Lessons Learned

Introduction — Project Scope & Planning

Before starting development of Rumble Smash, I defined a precise scope for the project and identified the main challenges that I would have to face during production, essential steps in order to meet the May deadline. To structure this pre-production phase I immediately drew up a Game Design Document (GDD), using it as a reference to define both the objectives of the project, to have a complete overview of the project, both to be able to estimate the completion time of each component that animated it, so that it could be delivered by deadline.

The GDD collected the fundamental elements of the experience that I wanted to create, including:

  • Concept and vision of the project, defining the idea at basis of the game and the experience I wanted to offer the player.

  • Core gameplay mechanics, examined in depth and compared with the others, in order to be able to evaluate the meaning of the designed mechanics.

  • Win/Lose Conditions, defining the conditions according which resulted in success or failure for the player during a game.

  • Art Direction and Art Style, establishing a direction coherent visual to maintain during ALL the pipeline production. It didn't go very well :(.

In parallel to the GDD I initialized a workspace on Notion within which I created a roadmap based on milestone, supporting me with the game design painted in the GDD.

Each milestone, each made up of features and objectives to reach, represented nothing more than a vertical slice of the project, reaching a new milestone also reached a new build valid and playable of the project, with features additional.

WHY?

I chose to structure the pre-production phase through GDD and a dedicated workspace on Notion because, even before tackling the implementation issues, I needed to define with precisely what I should have developed, what experience I wanted provide the player with the coherence and sense of the mechanics ideate.

The GDD editorial team allowed me to transform the idea initial into concrete milestones in the roadmap on Notion, distinguishing what was really needed for the vertical slice from secondary features that could have compromised the compliance with the deadline.

GDD and Notion therefore had two complementary functions: the the first defined what and why to build, while the second defined how to divide and verify development over time.

Al I had called them EPIC at some time to fit in with the workflow that uses Jira.
